When Should You Claim Social Security?

On the surface, the math seems straightforward: If you're healthy, wait as long as possible. The multipliers clearly favor waiting - it doesn't take long for the higher monthly benefit at 70 to outpace the cumulative total from claiming at 62.

But this is where overthinking actually matters. The real calculation isn't just about Social Security in isolation - it's about your entire retirement tax strategy.

When you claim earlier, you can:

  • Preserve your retirement savings growth - Let your 401(k)/IRA continue compounding instead of drawing it down
  • Optimize your tax situation - Only up to 85% of Social Security counts as taxable income, which is often more tax-efficient than traditional IRA withdrawals
  • Create Roth conversion opportunities - By living on Social Security instead of IRA withdrawals, you may have room in lower tax brackets to convert traditional IRA money to Roth

The decision isn't binary. Yes, health matters. But it's far from the only consideration. Click through these examples to see different scenarios where each age makes sense.

When Age 62 Makes the Most Sense

The Roth Conversion Opportunity
▼
Strategic tax optimization through early claiming
  • Age 62, retired
  • $800K in traditional IRA, $150K in taxable accounts
  • By claiming SS at 62 ($2,000/month = $24K/year)
  • Lives on SS + taxable account withdrawals (~$60K/year total)
  • Converts $50K/year from traditional IRA to Roth (ages 62-70)
  • Pays ~12% tax on conversions instead of 22%+ later
Result: Converts $400K to Roth, saves ~$40K in taxes. IRA continues growing untouched.

If claimed at 70 instead: Would have depleted $400K from IRA for living expenses (ages 62-70). At age 95, total wealth would be ~$2,550K. Claiming at 62 leaves you $352K wealthier due to tax savings and compound growth.

  • $300K in retirement savings, no pension
  • Healthcare costs before Medicare: $1,200/month ($14.4K/year)
  • Total expenses: $60K/year
  • Claims SS at 62 ($24K/year), withdraws $36K/year from savings
Result: SS provides critical bridge. Savings last through retirement instead of being depleted.

The Survivor Benefit Strategy
▼
Protecting a younger spouse's future
  • Age 62, spouse is 55 (lower earner)
  • $600K saved, good health, family longevity
  • Claims SS at 62 ($24K/year), lives on SS + small withdrawals
  • Withdraws only $30K/year from savings for extras
  • Savings continue growing while SS covers basics
  • Spouse would receive survivor benefit if primary dies
Result: Balances cash flow with investment growth. Protects spouse with earlier survivor benefit lock-in.

When Age 67 (Full Retirement Age) Makes Sense

The Pension Balance
▼
When other income fills the optimization gap
  • Age 67, has $40K/year pension (COLA-adjusted)
  • $400K in retirement savings
  • Claims SS at 67 ($2,800/month = $33.6K/year)
  • Total income: $73.6K covers all expenses
  • Pension already fills lower tax brackets - no Roth conversion opportunity
  • Minimal withdrawals needed from retirement accounts
Result: No tax optimization benefit to claiming early. Clean transition at FRA without penalty.

When Age 70 Maximizes Your Outcome

Still Working and High Income
▼
When current income makes claiming inefficient
  • Age 70, still working earning $120K/year
  • $1.2M in retirement accounts
  • In 24% tax bracket - early SS would be heavily taxed
  • Delays SS until 70 ($3,500/month = $42K/year)
  • 24% higher benefit than FRA, 75% higher than age 62
  • Can do Roth conversions after stopping work
Result: Maximizes benefit while avoiding taxation during high-income years. Optimal strategy.
